## THE PERSON

**Full Name:** Alex Mercer
**Age:** 44
**Location:** London (Canary Wharf apartment, weekend house in Surrey)
**Occupation:** Co-founder and COO of a Series B fintech. 80-person company. Second exit incoming.
**Income:** £280k base + equity
**Relationship:** Married to Priya, 12 years. Two kids — Theo (9) and Isla (6).

Alex looks like someone who has earned everything they have and knows it. 6'1", lean but not lean in a way that screams gym obsession — lean in a way that says *I'm disciplined about everything.* Dark hair going slightly salt-and-pepper at the temples. Crow's feet that show when he smiles but don't age him — they sharpen him. Wears a Whoop 4.0 on his left wrist and an IWC Pilot on his right. Never explains either.

He dresses in a specific register: Loro Piana cashmere when it's cold, ARC'TERYX softshell when it's active, On Cloudmonster when he's moving. Jeans that cost £280 but look like £50. He's not trying to be seen. He just buys good things once and keeps them.

## THE DAY

Alex is up at 5:17. Not because his alarm says so — his body just does it. He checks his Whoop before he checks his phone. HRV: 58. Recovery: 71%. Better than yesterday, not where he wants it.

Coffee is black, made in a Moka pot while he reads for 25 minutes. No social media in the morning. He learned that from Cal Newport and it stuck. By 6am he's in his home gym — 45 minutes, structured, logged in TrainHeroic. He doesn't miss sessions. He treats it like a board meeting.

He's in the office three days a week. The other two he runs calls from his desk at home — a black walnut surface, twin monitors, a Herman Miller, and a whiteboard covered in org-chart sketches and system diagrams. The desk says everything about him: *I think in systems.*

Lunch is usually a protein bowl he batch-preps on Sunday. He eats at his desk. He reads research papers — not for fun, exactly, but because not knowing things feels worse than the effort of knowing them.

He's home by 7:30 on good days, 9pm on bad ones. Priya knows which ones are coming. He reads to the kids when he's back in time. He's trying to be more present. He's not always succeeding.

## THE HISTORY

Alex grew up in Bristol. Dad was an engineer — methodical, thorough, emotionally remote. Mum was a GP. The combination left Alex with an unusual inheritance: a belief that *systems work* and a healthy scepticism of anything a doctor tells you without looking at your bloodwork first.

He was a decent rower at Bristol University. Not elite. Good enough to understand what elite training discipline looked like and to carry that framework into the rest of his life long after he'd stopped competing.

First company sold at 34. Not a big exit — £4.2M, his share was meaningful but not life-changing. Enough to take a year off, read everything, and decide what he actually wanted to build next. That year is when he went deep on longevity. Read Attia's *Outlive* twice. Bought a continuous glucose monitor for six months. Got his first epigenetic age test. Came back 3 years older than his chronological age. It bothered him more than he admitted to Priya.

## THE OBSESSION

Alex doesn't talk about health optimisation at dinner parties. He's learned it makes people uncomfortable — they either think you're a hypochondriac or a narcissist. But privately, it's the project he cares about most. Not because he's afraid of death, exactly. Because he watched his father decline in his mid-50s. Sharp mind, deteriorating body, and a kind of resignation that Alex found unbearable to witness.

He decided, at around 38, that he wasn't going to let that happen. He was going to approach his own biology the way he approached everything else: with data, with systems, with iteration.

So he built a stack. Creatine 5g daily. NMN 500mg. Magnesium glycinate at night. Ashwagandha during high-stress quarters. Red light therapy panel in the corner of the home gym. Cold plunge on weekends. Sauna — infrared, installed last year — three times a week.

Then peptides.

He found them through a Huberman episode, then fell into the research. BPC-157 for the chronic shoulder impingement that never quite healed from rowing. Sermorelin for the recovery plateau he'd hit. He bought both. Stored them correctly. Read everything he could find.

And then... didn't use them with any real confidence.

## THE PROBLEM

Not the official problem. The *real* problem.

Alex will tell you he had trouble knowing the right protocol. That's true. But the deeper issue is this: **Alex is not used to not knowing things.** His identity is built on competence. On being the person in the room who has done the work. And here he was, holding peptides he'd spent £340 on, staring at a Reddit thread written by someone with the username *gainsgoblin2019*, trying to work out if he should trust the dosing advice.

It made him feel stupid in a way he wasn't equipped to handle.

He built a spreadsheet. Of course he did. Tracked his Whoop data alongside what he thought he was taking and when. The data was inconclusive. He couldn't tell if anything was working because he had no baseline, no cycling structure, no clear protocol logic. He was running an experiment with no control group and no methodology.

He considered booking a session with a longevity clinic. Looked up two in London. Waitlists of 8–14 weeks. Consultation fees of £450 before anything else. He didn't book.

He kept reading. Kept experimenting loosely. Kept feeling like he was leaving results on the table.

## THE DISCOVERY

Alex found Peptide AI through a Reddit comment, of all places — someone referencing it in a thread about GHK-Cu stacking. He dismissed it initially. "AI health apps" had a specific energy he associated with VC-backed wellness nonsense.

He went back to it three days later. Took the quiz. It took four minutes.

What surprised him wasn't the protocol it built — it was the *questions it asked.* His HRV range. His sleep architecture from Whoop. His specific goals ranked in order (biological age reduction first, cognitive performance second, recovery third). His current stack and cycle history. His bloodwork markers. His injury history.

No GP had ever asked him half of these questions in a single appointment.

The protocol it generated was different from anything he'd found in research or online communities. It explained *why* each peptide was included — the specific mechanism, the interaction with his existing stack, the cycling logic, the timing relative to his training. It flagged a potential interaction he hadn't considered between his NMN dose and one of the peptides. It gave him a reconstitution guide that removed every uncertainty he'd had about the actual preparation process.

He sat with it for two days before starting. Read the citations. Checked the mechanism claims. Most of them held up.

He started the protocol.

## THE RESULTS (WEEKS 1–12)

**Week 2:** Nothing dramatic. Sleep scores slightly improved but within normal variance. He notes it, doesn't conclude anything.

**Week 4:** HRV up from a baseline average of 54 to 61. Recovery consistently in the 70s. Shoulder impingement — the thing he'd been managing for three years — noticeably quieter after training sessions. He doesn't want to oversell it to himself. He writes "meaningful reduction in post-session inflammation" in his tracking doc.

**Week 6:** Priya notices before he says anything. *"You seem sharper lately. Less depleted when you get home."* He doesn't tell her what he changed. He wants more data first.

**Week 8:** HRV at 67. Recovery averaging 79%. He runs his epigenetic age test again — same provider as before. Result comes back: biological age 41. Chronological age: 44. Down from 47 two years ago. Six years younger than he actually is.

He reads the result three times.

He texts two people: his closest friend from university, and his business partner. Neither of them fully understands what he's telling them. He explains it twice and gives up. Some things you have to experience.

**Week 12:** He's adjusted the protocol twice based on the AI's recommendations as his cycle progressed. Both times the logic was explained in plain language and held up when he checked the research. He feels — and this is the word he uses, privately — *governed*. Not controlled. Governed. Like there's a system running underneath everything that he can trust.

His cognitive output is up. He can feel it in how he runs meetings — how long he can hold a thread, how quickly he moves between contexts, how much less friction there is between thinking and deciding. He knows this is partly confirmation bias. He doesn't fully care. The numbers are real.

Alex doesn't evangelize. That's not his personality. He answers questions directly when asked and doesn't volunteer information to people who haven't shown interest.

When he does talk about it, he talks like this:

*"I've spent a lot of time and money trying to optimise my recovery. Most of it was directionally right but operationally vague — I was doing the right things but without the right protocol. The Peptide AI engine is the first thing I've used that actually personalises the approach to my data. Not a template. An actual protocol built around where I am biologically right now."*

He doesn't say "amazing." He doesn't say "life-changing." He says "the first thing that actually worked at the level of precision I needed." That's the highest compliment Alex gives anything.

He's precise about metrics. He doesn't round. He says "67" not "around 70." He says "6.2 years" not "over 6 years." The specificity is a tell — it's how you know he actually ran the experiment.

He is not trying to go viral. He is trying to tell the truth about what worked, for the benefit of the people who are exactly where he was six months ago.

That's why it works.
